## Why tracking helps

Brain fog is hard to describe and harder to remember. On a bad day, it can feel like it has always been this way. On a good day, it's easy to forget how bad it got. Memory is a poor record of something that affects memory.

A simple log fixes that. It turns "I've been foggy lately" into something you can see: which days were worse, what came before them, and whether anything you tried made a difference.

Tracking doesn't diagnose anything. It doesn't fix anything on its own either. What it does is give you, and your doctor, a clearer picture to work from.

<Callout type="info">
Most people need about two to three weeks of regular logs before patterns start to show. A month is better. Gaps are fine — a patchy log is still far more useful than no log.
</Callout>

## Keep it small

The biggest mistake is making the log too detailed. If tracking takes ten minutes, you'll stop on exactly the days that matter most — the foggy ones.

A good brain fog log takes seconds. At minimum, record:

- **A fog rating.** A simple scale, like 1 to 5, is enough. Don't overthink the number. Your gut answer is the right one.
- **The time.** Fog often shifts during the day. Morning fog and afternoon fog can have different causes.

That's the core. Everything else is optional and can be added later.

## What to record alongside it

Once the rating habit sticks, add a few factors. Pick the ones most likely to matter for you. You don't need all of them.

- **Sleep.** How long, and how rested you felt. Poor sleep is one of the most common fog triggers.
- **Stress.** A rough rating is enough. High-stress days often show up as fog the next day, not the same day.
- **Food and drink.** Big meals, sugar, alcohol, and caffeine are worth noting. So is skipping meals or not drinking enough water.
- **Activity.** Exercise can clear fog for some people. For people with ME/CFS or long COVID, overexertion can cause a crash a day or two later.
- **Menstrual cycle.** Fog often follows hormonal shifts, especially in perimenopause.
- **Weather.** Some people notice fog when air pressure drops or humidity rises.
- **Medication and supplements.** Note any changes in dose or timing.

<Callout type="tip">
Start with your fog rating plus two factors you already suspect. Add more only if the first ones don't explain much. A short list you keep up beats a long one you abandon.
</Callout>

## When to log

Consistency matters more than timing. Some options that work:

- **Once a day, same time.** Simple and easy to remember. Evening works well, so you can rate the day as a whole.
- **Twice a day.** Morning and evening. Useful if your fog changes a lot during the day.
- **Whenever you notice it.** Log the moment fog hits, or the moment it lifts. Good for spotting triggers like meals or long screen sessions.

If you forget a day, skip it. Don't try to backfill from memory — that's the unreliable data you're trying to avoid.

## How to read your log

After a few weeks, look back. You're not looking for proof. You're looking for hints worth testing.

- **Compare your worst days.** What did they have in common? Short sleep the night before? A stressful day two days earlier? A heavy lunch?
- **Compare your best days.** What was different? Good clear days are just as informative as bad ones.
- **Look for delays.** Many triggers act with a lag. Alcohol, poor sleep, and overexertion often show up the next day or the day after.
- **Watch for slow trends.** A gradual rise in fog over weeks can point to something like a medication change, a new season, or a thyroid or iron issue worth checking.

<Callout type="warning">
A pattern in your log is a clue, not a diagnosis. If you notice fog getting steadily worse, or it comes with new symptoms like numbness, confusion, or severe headaches, talk to your doctor promptly.
</Callout>

## Test one change at a time

Once you have a suspect, test it. Change one thing for one to two weeks and keep logging.

- Go to bed 30 minutes earlier.
- Swap a sugary breakfast for one with more protein.
- Cut alcohol for two weeks.
- Take a short walk after lunch.
- Plan rest days after busy days.

Then compare. Did your average fog rating drop? Were the bad days less bad? Changing several things at once feels efficient, but you won't know which one helped.

## Paper, spreadsheet, or app

Any method works if you keep using it.

- **Paper** is quick and calm, but hard to analyse. Spotting a two-day lag by flipping pages is tough.

## Bring it to your appointment

A log is one of the most useful things you can bring to a doctor. It turns a vague complaint into a timeline: how often, how bad, and what seems linked. That can speed up decisions about tests and next steps.

Keep a short summary ready: how long the fog has lasted, your typical rating, your worst stretches, and anything that seemed to help or hurt. FoggyBrain Pro can export a doctor-ready PDF of this, but a handwritten page works too.

## The short version

- Rate your fog daily on a simple scale. Keep it under a minute.
- Add two or three factors you suspect.
- Wait two to four weeks before drawing conclusions.
- Look for lags, not just same-day links.
- Test one change at a time.
- Share what you find with your doctor.

Tracking won't clear the fog by itself. But it can show you where to look — and that is often the first real step forward.
