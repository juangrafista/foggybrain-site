## Exercise changes the brain's structure

Aerobic exercise reliably increases levels of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — sometimes called "Miracle-Gro for the brain". BDNF promotes neurogenesis (the growth of new neurons) in the hippocampus, the brain region most critical for memory and learning. It also strengthens existing synaptic connections.

A single bout of moderate aerobic exercise produces measurable improvements in attention, working memory, and executive function that last 1–2 hours. With regular training, these benefits compound into lasting structural changes — the hippocampus measurably increases in volume with sustained aerobic exercise.

## What kind and how much?

Aerobic exercise (walking briskly, cycling, swimming, running) has the strongest evidence for cognitive benefits. 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week — the standard public health recommendation — is sufficient to produce meaningful cognitive improvements in most people.

Resistance training also benefits cognition, particularly executive function, and has additive effects when combined with aerobic exercise. Even a single session of resistance training has been shown to improve memory consolidation in the hours following the session.

<Callout type="tip">
If time is limited, prioritise a 20–30 minute walk on days you're experiencing brain fog. Many people report this produces a noticeable short-term improvement in clarity, likely via increased cerebral blood flow and BDNF release.
</Callout>

## Exercise and sleep interact

Regular exercisers consistently report better sleep quality — shorter time to fall asleep, more slow-wave sleep, and feeling more rested. Since sleep is the single strongest lever for brain fog, this compounding effect makes exercise doubly valuable.

<Callout type="warning">
Vigorous exercise within 2–3 hours of bedtime can delay sleep onset in some people by elevating core body temperature and cortisol. Morning or afternoon sessions are preferable if you're sensitive to this.
</Callout>

## Even light movement helps

- Breaking up prolonged sitting with short walks reduces cognitive fatigue
- A 10-minute walk after meals helps blunt blood sugar spikes
- Even gentle yoga or stretching reduces cortisol and improves mood
- The dose-response is roughly linear — more is better, but some is far better than none
