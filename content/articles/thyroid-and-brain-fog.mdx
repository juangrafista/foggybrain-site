## A small gland with an outsized effect on thinking

The thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in your neck, and it's easy to overlook as a cause of brain fog — but thyroid hormone regulates metabolism in nearly every cell in your body, including neurons. When thyroid levels drift too low or too high, cognitive symptoms are often among the first things people notice, sometimes well before more "classic" symptoms like weight change or fatigue become obvious.

<Callout type="info">
Thyroid disorders are common and underdiagnosed — an estimated 1 in 8 women will develop a thyroid condition in her lifetime, and many cases go unrecognized for years because symptoms build slowly and overlap with ordinary tiredness or stress.
</Callout>

## Two directions, two different kinds of fog

- **Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid)** is the more common cause of brain fog. Low thyroid hormone slows overall metabolic activity, including in the brain — this shows up as slowed thinking, forgetfulness, difficulty finding words, and a heavy, sluggish mental quality. It's often paired with fatigue, weight gain, feeling cold, and dry skin.
- **Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid)** causes a different, more agitated kind of fog — racing but unfocused thoughts, trouble concentrating, restlessness, and difficulty completing tasks despite feeling wired. It's often paired with a racing heart, weight loss, heat intolerance, and anxiety.
- **Hashimoto's thyroiditis**, an autoimmune condition and the most common cause of hypothyroidism, can cause cognitive symptoms that fluctuate with antibody levels and inflammation, sometimes even before thyroid hormone levels fall clearly outside the normal range.

## Why it's easy to miss

Thyroid-related fog builds gradually, often over months, which makes it easy to attribute to stress, aging, or simply being busy. Standard checkups don't always include thyroid testing unless you ask, and mild or "subclinical" thyroid dysfunction — where hormone levels are just outside normal range but not dramatically so — can still meaningfully affect cognition while producing borderline lab results that are easy to dismiss.

<Callout type="warning">
Thyroid symptoms are frequently misattributed to depression, perimenopause, or ordinary aging, especially in women over 40 — all of which can coexist with thyroid dysfunction and share overlapping symptoms. If brain fog has crept in gradually alongside fatigue, weight changes, or mood shifts, a thyroid panel is a reasonable, low-cost thing to rule out.
</Callout>

## What testing actually involves

A basic thyroid panel is a simple blood draw — no special preparation needed. It typically measures TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone, the most common first screen), and often free T4 and free T3 if TSH is abnormal or symptoms are strong despite a "normal" TSH. If Hashimoto's is suspected, thyroid antibody tests (TPO antibodies) can help identify autoimmune involvement even when hormone levels still look normal.

## What helps

- **Getting tested if you haven't been**, especially if fog has appeared alongside fatigue, temperature sensitivity, weight changes, or hair thinning — this is genuinely one of the more fixable causes of brain fog on this list.
- **Consistent follow-up if you're already on thyroid medication.** Levothyroxine and similar medications require dose adjustments over time, and being on treatment doesn't guarantee your levels are currently well-controlled — periodic re-testing matters.
- **Taking thyroid medication consistently and correctly** — timing (usually on an empty stomach, away from coffee, calcium, and iron supplements) meaningfully affects absorption and can leave levels under-corrected even when the dose looks right on paper.
- **Being persistent if symptoms don't match "normal" labs.** Reference ranges are population averages, not a perfect fit for every individual — if fog and other symptoms are strong and thyroid levels are borderline, it's reasonable to ask about retesting or referral to an endocrinologist.

<Callout type="tip">
Thyroid dysfunction tends to produce a fairly steady, background level of fog rather than day-to-day spikes — which makes it easy to normalize as "just how I feel now." Logging your fog over a few weeks in FoggyBrain, especially before and after a dose adjustment, can help make an otherwise slow, hard-to-notice change visible.
</Callout>

## Worth ruling out, not something to guess at

Thyroid dysfunction isn't the most common cause of brain fog, but it's one of the more clearly testable and treatable ones — a single blood test can meaningfully change the picture. If fog has been building gradually and doesn't have an obvious explanation, it's a reasonable thing to bring up at your next appointment, even if nothing else points specifically to your thyroid.
