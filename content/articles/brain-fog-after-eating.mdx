## A common, frustrating pattern

You eat lunch. Half an hour later, your head feels heavy. Words come slowly. You read the same email three times. By mid-afternoon, it lifts — or it doesn't.

Feeling a bit slower after a big meal is normal. But if fog after eating is regular, strong, or getting in the way of your day, it's worth looking at more closely. There are several possible causes, and some are easy to change.

<Callout type="info">
Feeling tired after eating is sometimes called "postprandial somnolence" — the classic post-lunch dip. Fog after meals overlaps with it, but can also involve trouble focusing, memory lapses, and a heavy-headed feeling.
</Callout>

## Common causes

### Blood sugar swings

Meals high in refined carbs and sugar — white bread, pastries, sweet drinks, big portions of pasta — can raise blood sugar quickly. Your body responds with insulin. In some people, blood sugar then falls fast, and that dip can bring fog, shakiness, irritability, and hunger.

This is often called a "sugar crash." It tends to show up one to three hours after eating.

### Meal size

Large meals take more work to digest. Blood flow shifts toward the gut, and the body's rest-and-digest system becomes more active. The result can be drowsiness and slower thinking, whatever you ate.

### The natural afternoon dip

Your body clock has a natural dip in alertness in the early afternoon. Lunch often lands right in it. So part of your post-lunch fog may be timing, not food.

### Food sensitivities and intolerances

Some people notice fog after specific foods. Common suspects include gluten, dairy, and certain additives.

- **Coeliac disease** is an autoimmune reaction to gluten. Brain fog is a commonly reported symptom, alongside gut problems, fatigue, and low iron.
- **Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity** is less well understood, but some people report fog after eating gluten without having coeliac disease.
- **Lactose intolerance** mainly causes gut symptoms, but the discomfort can make focus harder.

<Callout type="warning">
Don't cut gluten out before being tested for coeliac disease. The blood test needs you to be eating gluten to be accurate. Talk to your doctor first if you suspect it.
</Callout>

### Alcohol with meals

A glass of wine at lunch can have a bigger effect than you'd expect, especially combined with a large meal and the afternoon dip.

### Other medical causes

Less commonly, fog after eating can be linked to:

- **Reactive hypoglycaemia** — blood sugar dropping too low after meals.
- **Diabetes or prediabetes**, which affect how your body handles blood sugar.
- **Histamine intolerance**, which some people link to aged, fermented, or leftover foods.
- **POTS and other autonomic conditions**, where digestion can draw blood away from the brain and worsen symptoms.

If fog after meals comes with sweating, shaking, a racing heart, or fainting, see your doctor.

## What helps

### Build balanced meals

Pair carbs with protein, fat, and fibre. This slows how fast sugar enters your blood and can soften the crash.

- Add eggs, yoghurt, beans, fish, or chicken to meals.
- Choose whole grains over refined ones.
- Add vegetables to bulk up meals without a big sugar load.
- Eat fruit whole instead of as juice.

### Eat smaller, more often

If big meals knock you out, try smaller portions spread through the day. A lighter lunch with a snack mid-afternoon often works better than one large meal.

### Change the order you eat in

Some research suggests eating vegetables and protein before carbs in the same meal can reduce the blood sugar spike. It's a small change worth trying.

### Move after eating

A gentle 10 to 15-minute walk after a meal helps your muscles use glucose. It can reduce the spike and the dip that follows. If you have ME/CFS, keep this very light and within your limits.

### Watch the drinks

Sweet drinks, juice, and alcohol can all add to the crash. Water or unsweetened drinks are a safer default. Dehydration can also cause fog, so keep drinking through the day.

### Mind the timing

If your fog is mostly early afternoon, the body clock may be part of it. Try a lighter lunch, daylight, and a short walk around that time.

<Callout type="tip">
Caffeine right after lunch can mask the dip. But caffeine late in the day can hurt your sleep — and poor sleep is one of the strongest fog triggers. A cut-off around early afternoon works for many people.
</Callout>

## How to find your triggers

Food-related fog is very personal. A meal that knocks one person out does nothing to another. The only way to know is to track it.

For two to three weeks, note:

- What you ate, roughly. No need to weigh anything.
- When you ate.
- Your fog rating one to three hours later.
- Anything else that might matter, like sleep the night before.

Then look for patterns. Are the foggy afternoons after heavy lunches? After certain foods? Or after poor nights, regardless of food?

[FoggyBrain](/) makes this quicker. You rate your fog in two taps and log factors like sugar alongside it — or add your own, like "big lunch". Over time, it shows which factors tend to come before your foggier moments — so you can test changes with some evidence behind them.

## Test one change at a time

Once you have a suspect, change one thing for one to two weeks:

- A protein breakfast instead of a sweet one.
- A smaller lunch.
- A walk after meals.
- No alcohol at lunch.

Keep logging. If your post-meal fog improves, you've found something useful. If not, move on to the next idea.

## The short version

- Some post-meal sluggishness is normal. Regular, strong fog is worth looking into.
- Blood sugar swings, large meals, and the afternoon dip are the most common causes.
- Food sensitivities, including coeliac disease, can play a role — get tested before cutting foods out.
- Balanced meals, smaller portions, and a short walk after eating often help.
- Tracking fog against meals is the clearest way to find your personal triggers.

Food is one of the easier things to experiment with. A few weeks of notes can show you what your brain does and doesn't do well with.
