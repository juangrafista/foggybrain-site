## Why it's hard to explain

Brain fog is hard to put into words, especially when you're foggy. In a short appointment, it can come out as "I just don't feel sharp." That's true, but it's vague. It's easy for it to be filed under stress or tiredness.

You don't need medical language. You need a clear picture. A bit of preparation can make the difference between "let's wait and see" and a useful plan.

<Callout type="info">
Brain fog isn't a diagnosis. It's a description of symptoms. Your doctor's job is to find what's behind it — and the more specific you can be, the easier that is.
</Callout>

## Describe what actually happens

Instead of "brain fog," describe what it does to your day. Concrete examples help most.

- **Memory:** "I forget why I walked into a room several times a day."
- **Words:** "I lose words mid-sentence, or use the wrong one."
- **Focus:** "I read the same page three or four times."
- **Speed:** "Simple tasks at work take me twice as long."
- **Overload:** "I can't follow conversations in busy places."
- **Effort:** "After an hour of focus, I'm wiped out for the day."

Also mention how it affects your life. Missed work, mistakes, stopping driving, or dropping hobbies all show how serious it is.

## Know your timeline

Your doctor will likely ask:

- **When did it start?** A rough month is fine.
- **Did anything happen around then?** An illness, like COVID or flu? A new medication? Pregnancy, a big stressor, a change in sleep?
- **Is it constant or does it come and go?**
- **Is it getting better, worse, or staying the same?**
- **Are there good days and bad days?** What tends to happen before the bad ones?

This is where a log is worth a lot. Memory is unreliable for this — especially when memory is the thing that's affected.

## What to bring

A one-page summary is ideal. Include:

- **Your main symptoms**, with examples.
- **When it started**, and what was going on at the time.
- **How often and how bad.** For example, "fog 5 days a week, worst in the afternoon."
- **Other symptoms**, even ones that seem unrelated: fatigue, headaches, poor sleep, low mood, dizziness on standing, period changes, numbness.
- **Medications and supplements**, including doses.
- **What makes it better or worse**, if you've noticed anything.
- **What you've already tried.**

## Tests worth asking about

Your doctor will decide what's right for you. But it's reasonable to ask whether these have been considered:

- **Full blood count** — for anaemia.
- **Ferritin** — iron stores, which can be low before anaemia shows.
- **Vitamin B12 and folate.**
- **Thyroid function** — usually TSH, sometimes with free T4.
- **Blood sugar** — such as HbA1c.
- **Vitamin D.**
- **Kidney and liver function.**
- **Coeliac screening**, if you have gut symptoms or low iron.

Depending on your history, your doctor may also consider sleep problems like sleep apnoea, hormonal changes like perimenopause, mood conditions, or medication side effects.

## Questions to ask

- "What could be causing this?"
- "Which tests make sense, and what will they rule out?"
- "Could any of my medications be contributing?"
- "If the tests come back normal, what's the next step?"
- "Is a referral worth considering?" For example, to a neurologist, an endocrinologist, or a long COVID clinic.
- "What should I watch for that means I should come back sooner?"

## If you feel dismissed

Normal test results don't mean nothing is wrong. Conditions like long COVID, ME/CFS, and perimenopause often have normal routine bloods.

If you feel brushed off:

- **Be specific about impact.** "I've had to reduce my work hours" lands differently than "I feel foggy."
- **Ask for your concerns to be noted** in your record.
- **Ask what the plan is** if things don't improve.
- **Book a follow-up** rather than leaving it open.
- **Seek a second opinion** if you need to. That's a normal, reasonable step.

<Callout type="warning">
Some symptoms need urgent attention. Sudden confusion, trouble speaking, weakness or numbness on one side, a severe sudden headache, or fog after a head injury should be checked straight away — call emergency services if they come on suddenly.
</Callout>

## Make the appointment easier on yourself

Appointments can be tiring when you're foggy. A few things help:

- **Write your notes in advance.** Bring them on paper or your phone.
- **Put your top concern first.** Time is short.
- **Bring someone** if you can. They can listen and take notes.
- **Ask to repeat back the plan** at the end, so you leave with it clear.
- **Book a time of day** when your fog is usually lighter, if you know it.

## The short version

- Describe what fog does to your day, with concrete examples.
- Know when it started and what changed around then.
- Bring a one-page summary, including medications and other symptoms.
- Ask about common, testable causes like iron, B12, thyroid, and blood sugar.
- Normal results aren't the end of the conversation — ask what's next.
- A simple fog log makes your case clearer and easier to follow up.

You know your own mind better than anyone. Preparation helps your doctor see what you see.
