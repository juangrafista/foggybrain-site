## Two common, testable causes

When brain fog has no obvious explanation, two nutrients are worth checking early: iron and vitamin B12. Both are common deficiencies. Both can affect thinking. And both can be found with a simple blood test.

Neither one is the answer for everyone. But they're among the more fixable causes of brain fog, and they're easy to miss.

<Callout type="info">
Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world. B12 deficiency is also common, especially in older adults, vegans and vegetarians, and people taking certain long-term medications.
</Callout>

## Iron and the brain

Iron helps red blood cells carry oxygen around the body, including to the brain. It's also used to make brain chemicals like dopamine.

When iron runs low, the brain can get less oxygen and less of what it needs to work well. Common effects include:

- Trouble concentrating.
- Feeling mentally slow.
- Tiredness and low energy.
- Headaches.
- Feeling breathless on stairs or short walks.
- Cold hands and feet.
- Restless legs, especially at night.

### Low iron without anaemia

You can be low on iron before you become anaemic. Your body first uses up its iron stores, measured by a test called **ferritin**. Only later does haemoglobin, the anaemia marker, start to drop.

Some people have symptoms, including fog and fatigue, while ferritin is low but haemoglobin is still normal. If only haemoglobin is checked, this stage can be missed.

### Who's at risk

- People with heavy periods.
- People who are pregnant or have recently given birth.
- Vegetarians and vegans, since plant iron is absorbed less well.
- People with coeliac disease or other gut conditions.
- Frequent blood donors.
- Endurance athletes.

## B12 and the brain

Vitamin B12 is needed for healthy nerves and for making red blood cells. Low B12 can affect the brain and nervous system directly.

Common effects include:

- Brain fog and memory problems.
- Low mood or irritability.
- Fatigue.
- Pins and needles or numbness in hands and feet.
- Balance problems.
- A sore, smooth tongue.

<Callout type="warning">
B12 deficiency can cause nerve damage if left untreated for a long time. If you have numbness, tingling, or balance problems alongside fog, see your doctor soon rather than waiting.
</Callout>

### Who's at risk

- **Vegans and some vegetarians.** B12 comes almost only from animal foods, so supplements or fortified foods are usually needed.
- **Older adults.** Stomach acid and B12 absorption both tend to fall with age.
- **People with pernicious anaemia**, an autoimmune condition that blocks B12 absorption.
- **People taking metformin** for diabetes, especially long-term.
- **People on long-term acid-reducing medicines**, such as omeprazole and similar drugs.
- **People who've had stomach or bowel surgery.**

### Folate's role

Folate (vitamin B9) works closely with B12. Low folate can cause similar symptoms. Doctors often test both together.

## Which tests to ask about

If you've had brain fog for a while, it's reasonable to ask your doctor about:

- **Full blood count.** Checks for anaemia.
- **Ferritin.** Shows your iron stores. Ask for this specifically.
- **Vitamin B12.** Sometimes with extra tests, like active B12 or methylmalonic acid, if results are borderline.
- **Folate.**

These are often done alongside thyroid tests and other routine checks when fog is the main complaint.

<Callout type="tip">
Ask for your actual numbers, not just "normal." Ferritin and B12 both have wide reference ranges, and some people have symptoms near the low end. Your doctor can tell you whether a borderline result is worth acting on.
</Callout>

## Treatment and what to expect

Treatment depends on the cause, so a doctor should guide it.

- **Iron** is usually given as tablets. Some people need an infusion if tablets don't work or aren't tolerated. Iron levels can take months to rebuild, so energy and focus often improve gradually.
- **B12** may be given as tablets or injections, depending on the cause. If absorption is the problem, injections are often needed. Some people notice changes within weeks; nerve symptoms can take longer.
- **Finding the root cause matters too.** Low iron from heavy periods, or low B12 from a medication, needs the underlying cause addressed as well.

Don't start high-dose iron without testing. Too much iron can cause harm, and it can hide the cause of the deficiency.

## The short version

- Low iron and low B12 are common, testable causes of brain fog.
- You can be low on iron stores (ferritin) before you're anaemic.
- B12 deficiency is more likely if you're vegan, older, or on metformin or acid-reducing drugs.
- Ask your doctor about a full blood count, ferritin, B12, and folate.
- Treatment works gradually — tracking your fog helps you see the change.

If your fog has been around for a while with no clear cause, these tests are a simple, sensible place to start.
