## Acute vs. chronic stress

Short-term (acute) stress actually sharpens certain cognitive functions — attention narrows, decision-making speeds up, memory encoding of the stressful event is enhanced. This is the fight-or-flight response working as designed.

Chronic stress is different. Sustained elevation of cortisol — the primary stress hormone — damages the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (the regions responsible for memory, learning, attention, and executive function) while hyper-activating the amygdala (the brain's threat-detection centre). The result is impaired rational thinking, poor working memory, emotional reactivity, and persistent brain fog.

<Callout type="info">
Cortisol is not inherently bad — it has a healthy rhythmic pattern, highest in the morning (helping you wake and focus) and lowest at night. It's the dysregulation of this rhythm — flattened or chronically elevated — that causes problems.
</Callout>

## How stress causes brain fog

- Elevated cortisol impairs synaptic plasticity in the prefrontal cortex
- Chronic stress disrupts sleep architecture, particularly REM sleep
- Cortisol triggers neuroinflammation via microglial activation
- Stress depletes neurotransmitters: prolonged cortisol reduces serotonin and dopamine
- Cortisol raises blood sugar directly, contributing to the glucose spike-crash cycle

## Evidence-based stress reduction

Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) has the strongest body of evidence, consistently showing measurable reductions in cortisol, inflammatory markers, and self-reported brain fog after 8 weeks of practice. Even 10 minutes of daily meditation has been shown to strengthen prefrontal cortex connectivity and reduce amygdala reactivity.

Physiological sighing — a double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale through the mouth — is one of the fastest ways to activate the parasympathetic nervous system (rest-and-digest mode) and is supported by Stanford research as a real-time stress regulation tool.

## Practical approaches

- Daily mindfulness practice (10–20 min): guided apps or simple breath focus
- Cold exposure (cold shower, cold water face immersion): rapidly lowers cortisol and increases alertness
- Journaling: externalising worry reduces rumination and cognitive load
- Social connection: positive social interaction is one of the strongest buffers against chronic stress
- Time in nature: reduces cortisol and restores directed attention capacity
- Setting boundaries around work hours: cognitive restoration requires genuine downtime
